How to write a website brief that gets you the right site

By the bukkyai team · 6 min read

When you ask someone to build a website — a human designer or an AI — the first thing you give them is a brief. And the brief almost always goes one of two ways: it's either "I need a website for my bakery" (too thin to be useful), or a four-page document about brand values (too much to act on).

The best briefs sit in the middle. They're one or two sentences that answer three questions: what you do, who it's for, and what makes you different. That's it. Everything a good site needs — the pages, the words, the design — can be planned from those three answers.

The one-paragraph recipe: A [type of business] in [place] called [name]. [Who it's for]. [What's different about you].

Start with what you do, plainly

Most owners overcomplicate this. "We deliver holistic wellness solutions" is a sentence no customer has ever said. Instead: "A bakery in Austin called June & Oak. Wood-fired bread, coffee, and the same faces behind the counter every morning."

Plain words do two jobs: the site planner understands the business, and the copywriter can hear a voice. If you'd say it to a neighbor, write that down.

Say who it's for

Not "everyone" — everyone buys from no one. Is the bakery for weekend families or early-morning commuters? A site aimed at one of those looks completely different: families want tables and kids' menus; commuters want hours and a pickup counter.

You don't need a persona document. One clause — "regulars who know the neighborhood" — is enough for a good builder to make the right calls.

Say what makes you different

This is the part people leave out, and it's the part search engines and customers both notice. If every plumber in your city says "reliable, affordable, friendly," nobody stands out. But "we show up the same day, on time, in shoe covers" is a reason to call.

One honest detail beats five adjectives. It's also the seed of your homepage headline and your "why us" section.

What to leave out

  • Colors and fonts. A good builder picks those from your words — and can explain why.
  • Page lists. "I want home, about, services, contact" is the builder's job to plan, not yours.
  • Specifics you'll change anyway. Prices, phone numbers, exact copy — you'll edit these live.

Test it yourself

Write your sentence, then read it to someone who knows nothing about your business. If they can picture what you do and who you serve, you're done. Paste it into bukkyai, approve the plan, and the pages come back written in your voice.

That's the whole trick: a good website brief isn't a document. It's one honest paragraph.
