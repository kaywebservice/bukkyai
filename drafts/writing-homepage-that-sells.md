# Writing a homepage that sells without sounding salesy

_First published at [bukkyai.duckdns.org/blog/writing-homepage-that-sells](https://bukkyai.duckdns.org/blog/writing-homepage-that-sells)._

Most homepages don't fail because of bad design. They fail because the words are generic — "Welcome to our website" — or because they scream ("BEST DEALS! BOOK NOW!") until no one believes any of it.

There's a quieter way that works better: a homepage that assumes the visitor already wants what you offer, and just tells them *what you do, why it's for them, and what to do next.*

## One clear headline, spoken like a human

Your headline should say what you do, not what you're called. A visitor lands, scans for one second, and needs to know if they're in the right place.

- **Too clever:** "June & Oak — where every day starts golden."
- **Clear & warm:** "Wood-fired bread and coffee, baked before the city wakes."

The good one isn't more creative — it's more specific. It says what the business does (bread and coffee), how (wood-fired), and when (before the city wakes). That's a headline that earns its one second.

## One idea per section

Visitors scroll. Give them something new every screen: proof, then explanation, then the ask. A strong homepage layout runs roughly:

- **Headline + one line** — what you do.
- **A single strong image** — what you look like.
- **Two or three reasons** — why you're different.
- **Proof** — a review or a number ("1,400 loaves a week").
- **One call to action** — what to do next.

Notice what's not there: a paragraph about when you were founded, a photo of the team waving, and a second "call now!" button that competes with the first.

## One call to action, not five

Every button on a homepage is a vote against the others. "Order now" and "Book a table" and "View menu" all shouting at once reads as indecision. Pick the single action that matters most — the order, the booking, the contact — and make it the primary button. Give everything else a quieter link.

## Write like you're explaining it to a neighbor

The test for any homepage line: would you say it out loud? "We partner with local farms to bring you the freshest seasonal ingredients" — would you actually say that? No. You'd say "The vegetables come from the farm down the road."

Written language that sounds spoken is almost never a mistake. It's the difference between copy and a conversation.

**The takeaway:** specific beats clever, one idea per section beats an essay, and one clear button beats a menu of options. That's the whole homepage.
